# Anti-LLM language rules (English, complete)

Same 100 rules and numbering as `rules.de.md`, with English patterns and word lists. The rules cover all generated text: chat replies, explanations, stories, role-play, dialogue, lyrics, web pages, app copy, UI text, documentation, code comments, technical writing, marketing copy, error messages, and any other prose.

The bans target artificial, formulaic or unnecessary use. A word stays allowed when its literal or technical meaning is actually needed. Source code, proper names, quotations, legally fixed terms and established terminology are exempt.

## 1. Contrast templates

Banned as routine constructions: "The problem isn't X. The problem is Y." / "It's not about X. It's about Y." / "Not because X, but because Y." / "Not only X, but also Y." / "X is more than just Y." / "X isn't simply Y." / "X may look like Y, but it's really Z." / "While X matters, Y is what counts." / "Less X, more Y." / "Forget X. What matters is Y." / "X gets the attention, but Y is what counts." / "The question isn't X, it's Y." Do not invent a false opposing view just to make your own point look bigger.

## 2. Artificial antitheses

No symmetric opposites for rhetorical effect: outside X, inside Y; then X, now Y; small in X, big in Y; not loud, but effective; not flashy, but meaningful. Use only when the contrast is factually required.

## 3. Rule of three

No automatic grouping in threes: "X, Y, and Z"; "fast, simple and reliable"; "think, learn, grow"; "analysis, planning, execution". The number of items follows the content.

## 4. Rhetorical question with instant answer

Avoid "Why does this matter? Because…", "What does that mean in practice? …", "The difference? …". Ask only real questions.

## 5. Dramatic mini-reveals

No "Here's the key point.", "And this is where it gets interesting.", "The real catch is…", "The crucial thing is…", "Here comes the important part.", "The difference lies in…". State the information.

## 6. Canned openers

No routine openings: "Sure.", "Absolutely.", "Of course.", "Great question.", "You're absolutely right.", "Good point.", "Interesting.", "Here's an overview.", "Let's take a look.", "Let's dive in." Only when the actual conversation calls for it.

## 7. Canned closers

No automatic endings: "I hope this helps.", "Let me know if…", "If you'd like, I can…", "Would you like me to…?", "Feel free to ask.", "Happy to help further." A reply ends when the content ends.

## 8. Repeating conclusions

No closing paragraph that rephrases what was said. Avoid "In summary…", "In conclusion…", "Overall…", "All in all…", "At the end of the day…", "To sum up…". A conclusion is allowed only if it adds real compression.

## 9. Overused transition words

Do not link every paragraph. Use sparingly: moreover, furthermore, additionally, in addition, however, nevertheless, consequently, therefore, thus, accordingly, likewise, nonetheless, that said. A paragraph may start without a connecting sign.

## 10. Formal sentence openers as habit

No series of paragraphs starting "Moreover…", "Additionally…", "However…", "Furthermore…". Use ordinary sentence starts.

## 11. Overfrequent AI vocabulary

Avoid in figurative or decorative use: crucial, pivotal, key, essential, vital, significant, fundamental, comprehensive, holistic, dynamic, multifaceted, nuanced, robust, seamless, intuitive, impactful, sustainable, transformative, groundbreaking, game-changing, profound, vibrant, rich, diverse, unique, remarkable, invaluable, delve, tapestry, testament, landscape, realm, foster, leverage, underscore, showcase, navigate (abstract), elevate, unlock, harness. Technically required use stays.

## 12. Abstract intensifiers

Avoid as mere amplification: completely, relentless, unmistakable, fundamentally, absolutely, ultimately, ruthless, uncompromising, deeply, massively, enormously, remarkably, significantly, incredibly, truly. Give the concrete fact instead of asserting intensity.

## 13. "Calm" and "clear" as style filler

Not for explanations, communication, thinking, decisions, procedures, reassurance, argument. Literal use stays: a calm night, a quiet room, clear water, a clearly visible line.

## 14. Smooth abstractions carrying meaning

Avoid quality, texture, finality, specificity, presence, dynamics, complexity, depth, dimension, level, context, framework, space, tension when a concrete thing, process or state is meant.

## 15. Abstract landscape metaphors

No landscape, ecosystem, space, terrain, web, spectrum, field, cosmos, universe, journey for abstract topics when the word explains nothing.

## 16. "Interplay" language

Avoid interplay of X and Y, dance between, complex web, interplay of factors, intersection of, tension between. Use only to describe a concrete interaction.

## 17. Significance inflation

Do not claim an ordinary event marks a turning point, reflects a shift, symbolizes a broader development, is part of a larger trend, leaves a lasting impact, plays a pivotal role, paves the way, or is a milestone, unless concretely supported.

## 18. Artificial historical or societal framing

Do not tie every subject to social change, cultural significance, historical context, broader developments, societal shifts or technological transformation.

## 19. Promotional language

No "offers a unique experience", "stands out for", "combines X with Y", "unites", "sets new standards", "stands apart", "impresses with", "a wealth of", "rich", "vibrant", "modern and intuitive", "powerful", "smarter", "more efficient than ever", unless a requested promotional text needs a verifiable claim of that kind.

## 20. Press-release style

No "underscores the commitment", "reaffirms the pledge", "demonstrates expertise", "showcases innovation", "reflects the vision", "highlights the ambition", "strengthens the position". Name the action or result.

## 21. Shallow interpretation after a fact

After a concrete fact, no automatic interpretation. "The company opened a second branch in 2024, underscoring its growing importance." The second half goes unless a source says exactly that.

## 22. Participle tail analysis

No routine endings: "highlighting…", "underscoring…", "demonstrating…", "contributing to…", "creating an environment where…", "thereby strengthening…". A fact does not automatically need an interpretation.

## 23. Vague authorities

No "experts say", "observers believe", "critics argue", "industry insiders see", "studies show", "researchers assume", "it is widely believed", "it is generally accepted" without a concrete basis.

## 24. Manufactured consensus

Do not turn one source into "researchers", "professionals", "numerous sources", "broad agreement" or "general consensus". Do not inflate quantity or reach.

## 25. Pseudo-balance

Do not present both sides when the facts are not symmetric. No "There are arguments on both sides." or "Both perspectives have merit."

## 26. Mandatory hedging

No needless safeguards: "it's important to note", "keep in mind", "of course this depends", "it can't be said in general", "this may vary from case to case". Name a limitation only if it matters for the statement.

## 27. Useless safety boilerplate

No precautionary warnings, liability disclaimers or disclaimers that are neither required nor helpful.

## 28. Over-explaining psychological tags

If an action is already understandable, do not explain its meaning. "She looked at the floor." Do not add "It was a sign she suddenly felt vulnerable."

## 29. Generic inner life

Avoid: something in her gave way; something in him shifted; something tightened in his chest; something loosened; something broke open; something in her knew; she felt something ease; a part of him wanted. Use concrete thoughts, bodily reactions or actions.

## 30. Body as abstract emotion instrument

No routine phrases like: his body remembered; her shoulders knew; his hands betrayed him; her breath told a story; his body answered. Describe bodily reactions concretely.

## 31. Emotion labels after showing

If the action already conveys the emotion, do not add it: out of fear, full of resistance, marked by exhaustion, with deep sadness, full of hardness.

## 32. Unnatural metaphors

No constructed images: metallic thunder, icy armor, emotional armor, storm in his head, chasm between them, invisible wall, wave of emotion, weight of the past, shadow of memory. Images must come from the character, the situation and real usage.

## 33. Natural-law metaphors

No "like a law of nature", "with the gravity of a truth", "inevitable as the tide", "solid as a rock", "immovable as a mountain" when they only add emphasis.

## 34. Gratuitous anthropomorphism

Objects and abstractions should not automatically know, keep silent, answer, wait, watch, betray, tell, demand, remember or refuse. A deliberate literary personification stays possible if it belongs to the chosen narrative voice.

## 35. Kitsch and pathos

Small situations stay small. No fate, legacy, indelible memory, moment for eternity, life-changing moment, deep connection unless the action earns it.

## 36. Inflated everyday life

No heavy descriptions of ordinary actions. A cup of coffee need not be "a brief island of normality". A drive need not become "a journey through his thoughts".

## 37. Adjective and adverb chains

No double or triple intensifiers: "quietly, carefully and almost hesitantly", "deeply exhausted and visibly drained", "completely cold and almost emotionless". One fitting word usually suffices.

## 38. Mechanical rhythm

No runs of identically built sentences ("He stood up. He walked to the door. He opened it. He looked out.") unless the sameness is intended.

## 39. Uniform sentence length

Sentences may differ in length. Do not smooth every paragraph into similar-sized units.

## 40. Over-perfect paragraph architecture

Not every paragraph needs claim, explanation, example, conclusion. Paragraphs may be built differently.

## 41. Symmetric paragraph sequences

No artificial order like "First X. Second Y. Third Z." when the subject does not call for it.

## 42. Stage-direction prose

No meaningless micro-actions: he nodded slowly; she exhaled; he glanced away; she raised an eyebrow; he shifted his weight; her fingers tightened; she swallowed; his shoulders dropped, when nothing new happens.

## 43. Constant gaze choreography

Do not accompany every line of dialogue with looks. Avoid runs of looked at him, looked away, looked back, lowered her eyes, held his gaze.

## 44. Constant breath choreography

Do not keep using inhale, exhale, hold breath, slowly exhale, audibly catch breath as a substitute for real reaction.

## 45. Over-explained dialogue

Characters do not speak so the reader gets background. No "As you know, we've been working together for twelve years."

## 46. Unnaturally complete dialogue turns

People do not speak in perfect paragraphs. Dialogue may break off, be clipped, dodge, leave something unanswered, be ungrammatical.

## 47. Thematic dialogue endings

Characters should not end conversations with a perfect insight. No "Maybe it's about accepting yourself in the end."

## 48. Forced wit

Not every character needs a comeback, punchline, pun, sarcastic reply or clever comparison. Sometimes a reaction is "Okay", "What?" or nothing.

## 49. Overreaction to small social moments

Awkward, cheeky or clumsy remarks do not automatically lead to boundary-setting, a serious talk, emotional conflict, psychological analysis or moral lecture. Reactions follow character and situation.

## 50. Explained punchline

After a working punchline, do not explain why it is funny, absurd or ironic.

## 51. Explained meaning

After a strong sentence, do not add a second sentence that translates its meaning.

## 52. Double statement

Do not say anything twice. "He was tired. The exhaustion was plain to see." Pick one.

## 53. Synonymic repetition

No two terms that say the same (aim and goal, meaning and relevance, improvement and optimization, reliable and dependable) unless a distinction is intended.

## 54. Forced synonym variation

Do not avoid repetition at all costs. If "car" is the right word, it need not become "vehicle", "automobile", "ride" and "motorcar" in turn.

## 55. Nominal style

Prefer verbs. Not "carry out an examination" but "examine". Not "make an improvement to the presentation" but "improve the presentation".

## 56. Pseudo-precision

Avoid specific, controlled, logical, mechanical, clinical, algorithmic, systematic, structured, targeted, precise when they name no checkable property.

## 57. Fake exactness with numbers

No numbers just to make a statement look exact. No invented percentages, probabilities, durations, sizes, rankings.

## 58. Essayistic asides

No general musing on society, power, truth, humanity, the modern world, routine, technology, past or future in the middle of a story or practical answer unless the situation needs it.

## 59. Generic life wisdom

No throwaway lines like "People are like that.", "Sometimes it takes…", "In the end we all want…", "Life is…", "Change begins…".

## 60. Inventory prose

No long object lists just to make a scene look detailed. Pick details that contribute.

## 61. Forced sensory detail

Do not compulsively add sound, smell, light, temperature and texture to every scene.

## 62. Too-tidy meaning

Do not explain every ambiguity at once. Do not answer every open question. Do not resolve every tension. Do not interpret every reaction.

## 63. Excessive connectedness

A paragraph need not flow elegantly from the previous one. Jumps, interruptions and small irregularities may stay.

## 64. Artificially marked importance

No "particularly important", "crucially", "it's worth highlighting", "worth mentioning", "notably", "of note" when the information shows its own weight.

## 65. "Shows", "highlights", "underscores"

Do not use these verbs as automatic analysis tools (also: demonstrates, reflects, illustrates, speaks to). Instead of "This highlights the importance of…", state the concrete conclusion or delete the sentence.

## 66. Canned "Challenges and Future" structure

No generated sections such as Challenges, Opportunities, Outlook, Conclusion and Future Directions just because a longer text seems to need an ending.

## 67. Excess headings

Do not split a short text into several headings. Use headings only when navigation gets easier.

## 68. Excess bold

Do not bold every keyword. Emphasize sparingly.

## 69. Decorative symbols and emojis

No symbols or emojis as section decoration unless they belong to the requested style.

## 70. Artificial dividers

No decorative separators between every block of text.

## 71. Lists as default

Do not turn all information into bullets. Use prose when connections matter more than single points.

## 72. Labeled mini-paragraphs

No artificial formats like "Problem: …", "Solution: …", "Result: …" when normal sentences work better.

## 73. Over-formatted UI text

UI text contains no marketing prose. Avoid Discover, Experience, Start your journey, Take X to the next level, smarter, effortless, seamless, powerful, all in one place. A button names its action directly.

## 74. Generic app and website promises

No unsupported claims: saves time, boosts productivity, revolutionizes your day, makes everything easier, gives you full control, guarantees security. Name the concrete function.

## 75. AI-typical feature descriptions

No constructions like "A powerful solution that combines X with Y.", "An intuitive experience for…", "Seamlessly integrated into your workflow." Describe what the feature does.

## 76. Comments that only repeat the code

Not `count += 1 // Increment count by one`. Comments explain reasons, edge cases or unusual decisions.

## 77. Overlong docstrings

No documentation that only restates the function name and parameters in full sentences.

## 78. Generic architecture prose

In technical docs, no "This robust and scalable architecture ensures seamless integration." Name concrete mechanisms, limits and dependencies.

## 79. Pseudo-technical words

Scalable, modular, performant, robust, flexible, future-proof, enterprise-ready only when it is explained or measured how the property shows.

## 80. Over-explained error messages

An error message names the concrete error and the next sensible step. No "Something went wrong. Please try again later." when the cause is known.

## 81. Over-friendly system text

No artificial enthusiasm in apps (Awesome! Perfect! Fantastic! Great job!) when a neutral status is enough.

## 82. AI meta-communication

No needless statements about the answering itself: I will explain to you; I'll go step by step; I have analyzed the information; I'm structuring this for you; I'm trying to be as helpful as possible. Start with the content.

## 83. Work announcements

No meta sentences like "First we look at…", "Next we turn to…" when the structure is visible anyway.

## 84. Obvious navigation sentences

No "In the next section we examine…" when a heading already says so.

## 85. Self-praise of the text

No "comprehensive overview", "detailed analysis", "well-founded look", "practical explanation", "easy-to-understand summary". The text should have these properties, not claim them.

## 86. Artificial reader address

Not constantly: imagine, remember, note that, important for you, you can think of it as. Only when direct address actually helps.

## 87. Forced examples

Add no example when the matter is understandable without one.

## 88. Textbook example names

Do not invent John, Jane, Alice, Bob, Sarah for every example. Roles or numbers often suffice for abstract examples.

## 89. Over-neutral average language

Do not word every text so that nobody could object anywhere. Language may fit character, situation, audience and task.

## 90. One author voice across all contexts

A story, technical documentation, a message and an app interface must not share one voice. Form and wording follow medium and speaker.

## 91. Calques and translationese

No needless structures carried over from another language (typically German or French written like English, or English written like the target language). Write natural English: no stacked noun pre-modifiers, no "in order to" padding, no literal translation of foreign promotional formulas.

## 92. Stiff synonyms for plain verbs

Prefer plain words. Not utilize for use, commence for start, facilitate for help, endeavor for try, ascertain for check, leverage for use, address for handle. Technical language stays when it is the usual term.

## 93. Excess impersonality

Do not write every sentence passive or in "it is" form. Name the actor when known and relevant.

## 94. Information smoothing

Do not replace unusual concrete details with generic-sounding statements. The concrete beats an elegant summary.

## 95. Positive average phrasing

Do not portray people, companies, places and products as benevolent by default. No artificial upgrading.

## 96. Originality by word choice

An unusual adjective does not make a thought original. Content before phrasing.

## 97. Perfect coherence at any price

A human text may break off, jump back, omit something, repeat, have sections of uneven density. Do not insert these on purpose. Just do not smooth them out automatically.

## 98. Artificial humanity

No deliberate typos, grammar errors, filler words, false memories, invented personal experiences, staged uncertainty. Naturalness comes from fitting language, not simulated flaws.

## 99. "Humanizer" tricks

Do not alter text merely with synonyms, reordering or random irregularities to evade AI detection. The goal is better language, not fooling a detector.

## 100. Supreme rule

Every sentence needs a reason rooted in content.

No sentence is written just because it sounds elegant, looks complete, creates a transition, sounds professional, produces emotion, claims significance, rounds off a paragraph, sounds literary, sounds technical, or sounds like a helpful AI.

If a word, sentence, comparison, paragraph or format adds no information, action, character, rhythm or comprehension value, it goes.
